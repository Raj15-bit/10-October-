The big picture: fuel is the new shortage, and India pays through the rupee

America made a deal for the same Russian fuel it punishes India for buying, while the cost of the fuel war flowed into India's currency, interest rates and power bills.

In short
  • America agreed to import Russian diesel, weeks after a new law let it tax countries like India for buying Russian oil.
  • The real shortage is refined fuel, so the rupee, Indian interest rates and evening power prices all moved the wrong way.
  • Indian shares bounced on 9 October, but most stocks are still far below their highs while company profits are slowly improving.

The double standard India now has to live with

On 9 October President Trump said Russia would ship diesel "to the American and Global Marketplace", and the US Treasury lifted sanctions on Russian diesel until April 2027. Less than a month earlier he had signed the Graham sanctions law, which lets Washington put tariffs of up to 100% on the biggest buyers of Russian oil, India among them.

  • The law — the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, signed on 18 September, allows tariffs of up to 100% on the top buyers of Russian oil and gas.
  • The exception — General License 135 lets Russian diesel enter the US until 7 April 2027.
  • The trade talks — the US–India trade talks have stalled at what one China and India specialist calls a "plateau".

One view is that this is plain hypocrisy that will hurt US–India ties further, after 14 months of threats over Russian oil. The opposite view is that the diesel volumes are tiny and the deal is a pre-election price move, not a change of policy. Reading between the lines, both can be true: the volumes are small, but the legal power to punish India stays in place, so India's bargaining position gets worse, not better.

How the fuel bill reaches Indian pockets

The rupee ended the week at its weakest weekly close ever, near 97 per dollar. The Reserve Bank of India raised its policy rate on 7 October, tightened the daily cash rule for banks and then announced steps to stop speculation against the rupee. Section 02 covers these in detail.

Fuel shows up in other bills too. Evening electricity on the exchanges hit its price ceiling, aluminium cans got scarcer, and jet fuel is near record highs. So households and companies are paying for the same war in several places at once.

Price of money: the RBI tightens on 3 fronts to defend a rupee near 97

A rate rise, a stricter daily cash rule and curbs on currency bets came in the same week, while long-term borrowing costs keep rising across the rich world.

In short
  • The RBI raised its main rate and changed its stance to "calibrated tightening", which means more rises are possible.
  • It also forced banks to park more cash every day and put limits on bets against the rupee.
  • Around the world, long-term government borrowing costs keep climbing, and gold has beaten bonds by a huge margin.

What the RBI did on 7 October

The repo rate (the rate at which the RBI lends to banks overnight) went up by a quarter point. The vote on the rise was unanimous, but 2 of the 6 members wanted to keep a neutral stance. The RBI raised its growth forecast and its inflation forecast at the same time.

The quieter step: a stricter daily cash rule

Banks must keep 3% of deposits with the RBI as the cash reserve ratio (CRR), earning nothing. Until now they could hold as little as 90% of that on any one day, as long as the 2-week average came right.

From 16 October the daily floor is 99%, which drains about ₹4 lakh crore of spare cash. The RBI also plans to sell bonds worth about $2.58 billion to soak up money.

One view, from Investec, is that the change is mostly a signal, because banks kept more than the full amount on most days anyway. The opposite view is that the timing is the message. Reading between the lines, the RBI had exempted the large foreign-currency deposit inflows (FCNR(B), about $135 billion) from the CRR, so raising the CRR rate would not have caught that money. A stricter daily floor does. The same thing was done in July 2013, the last time the rupee was under heavy attack.

The rupee, and the rules to protect it

Rupees per US dollar, weekly

Points read from a weekly USD/INR chart in the material; the last point is the record weekly close of 96.78.

The rupee closed the week at 96.78 per dollar, its weakest weekly close ever, against 88.84 a year ago. Forex reserves fell for a fourth week in a row, and the RBI sold dollars through state-run banks. Then it announced 5 steps, which start on Monday 12 October.

  • Oil companies — the RBI will supply the full daily dollar needs of IOC, HPCL and BPCL, so their large buying leaves the open market.
  • Bet size — the limit for currency trades without a real underlying need falls from $100 million to $5 million.
  • Cash cover — banks must keep 20% of the rupee value in cash with the RBI on some trades above $2 million.
  • No rebooking — a cancelled rupee contract cannot simply be booked again.
  • One hedge only — firms must certify that the same exposure is not hedged with another bank.
  • Reserves — forex reserves fell $12.95 billion in the week to 2 October, to $734.6 billion.

One view, from a Motilal Oswal note, is that the steps help the rupee in the near term but do not fix the cause, which is costly oil, foreign selling and steady dollar demand. The opposite view is blunter: rules cannot hold back that much demand, the rupee is still testing 97, and the market will decide in the end. So the rupee now depends on oil and foreign flows more than on any circular.

Is money growing too fast?

US economist Steve Hanke says India's money supply (M3, cash plus bank deposits) is growing about 17% a year, far above the pace he thinks fits India's inflation goal, and that the RBI is "losing control". Reading between the lines, part of that jump is the same FCNR money landing as bank deposits, which the RBI is now trying to drain. The argument is open and sits in section 10.

Gold: the trade that beat bonds

Gold has risen about 53% since January 2025, while long-dated Japanese and European bonds lost money (section 09 has the heat map). China is the biggest buyer, and Russia's finance ministry is buying currency and gold 5 times faster than planned.

  • China — imported more than 1,000 tonnes this year and spent $158.8 billion in January–August, against $96.5 billion in all of 2025.
  • India — scrapped the bank tax (IGST) exemption on bullion imports on 8 October, after raising the gold and silver duty from 6% to 15% in May.
  • Russia — will buy 279.42 billion roubles of currency and gold from 7 October to 6 November, against 55.6 billion planned a month earlier.

One view reads gold's price action as a sign that bond yields have topped. The other view notes that gold peaked at $5,595 in January and is well below that. Reading between the lines, India is making gold imports dearer for the same reason it is defending the rupee: every dollar spent on gold is a dollar not available to pay for oil.

Oil, diesel and war: the refinery war decides fuel prices now

Russia has crude but not enough working refineries, the Gulf is a war zone again, and India's refiners are selling record amounts of diesel to Europe.

In short
  • The Russian diesel promised to America is small, under 9 days of US use.
  • Ukraine's drone strikes have shut about a third of Russia's refining, and Ukraine hit a Russian fuel terminal hours after the deal.
  • Indian refiners gain from the shortage, while airlines and fuel-heavy industries lose.

The diesel deal, measured

Russian deputy prime minister Alexander Novak gave the schedule: shipments start in October, grow in November and reach a million tonnes in December. A tonne of diesel is about 7.4 barrels.

The strikes moved from fuel storage to the main distillation units in April, and the pace never fell back. So Russia's refinery output dropped to its lowest since 2002, even though its crude exports stayed near records.

  • Capacity — 92 verified strikes on 27 refineries; 11 plants offline, about 37% of Russia's refining capacity.
  • Exports — Russian seaborne diesel exports fell from about 1 million barrels a day to about 80,000 in early August; an export ban runs to 31 October.
  • Margins — the gasoil crack (what refiners earn over crude) hit a record $79 a barrel on 1 September and passed $100 in late September.
  • Stocks — European diesel stocks at Amsterdam–Rotterdam–Antwerp are about 25% below last year.
  • Latest hit — the Omsk refinery, more than 1,500 miles from the border, was struck on 8 October.

A Kpler analyst put it simply: Russia does not have an export problem, it has a refinery problem. Reading between the lines, that is good news for anyone who owns working refineries outside the war zone, and India owns a lot of them.

India's refiners are the quiet winners

India's diesel exports reached about 620,000 barrels a day in September, a 12-month high. Europe took about 280,000 of those, triple August's level.

Reliance's export-only Jamnagar refinery sits in a special economic zone, so it does not pay the windfall tax of about $37 a barrel that other exporters pay.

So the same shortage that hurts the rupee also earns India dollars. The catch is that most of the gain goes to one exporter, while the import bill falls on everyone.

The Gulf is a war zone again

  • Riyadh airport — Houthi missiles hit King Khalid airport on 8 October (3 killed, including a Saudia pilot) and again on 10 October, when Terminal 3 was hit and 305 flights were cancelled.
  • Red Sea — Yemen's coastguard said at least 6 sailors died when the Houthis struck a ship.
  • Hormuz — Iran's Revolutionary Guards said they set an LPG tanker on fire; Iran has attacked 11 ships trying to pass in October.
  • Bab al-Mandab — a Saudi TV report said the Houthis laid mines there; this is not confirmed, and Saudi-backed forces claim gains on that coast.
  • Saudi gas — satellites saw a heat signal of about 105 MW near the Shedgum gas plant; the tool itself says no attack is confirmed.
  • Diplomacy — Vladimir Putin passed Iran's terms for ending the war to Trump in their call, the first time Moscow has played that role.

One view, from a geopolitical analyst, is that the Houthis have momentum and could control both shores of the Gulf of Aden with Somalia's Al-Shabaab, a route for about 30% of the world's container traffic. The opposite view is that Saudi-backed forces are gaining on the Red Sea coast. Reading between the lines, a summit for global investors in Riyadh on 26–29 October is now at risk, which matters for Gulf money that flows into Indian markets.

The price range nobody can narrow

BP chief executive Meg O'Neill gave 2 outcomes for next year: about $150 a barrel if the Strait of Hormuz stays shut, or about $50 if peace arrives on several fronts. She said the old shock absorbers, such as Russian and Iranian oil on ships and government stockpiles, are used up.

China's imports are 4 to 5 million barrels a day lower than a year ago, which helped.

BP is not sending its chartered ships into the Gulf, but it is looking at bidding for India's deep-sea oil blocks under the Samudra Manthan policy (₹84,000 crore of planned spending). So the war is pushing foreign oil companies towards Indian exploration, slowly.

Who else is paying

  • Airlines — Delta Air Lines cut its 2026 profit guide to $5.10–5.60 a share from $6.50–7.50; jet fuel is near $5 a gallon in New York and Los Angeles.
  • Hurricane Isaias — knocked out power to about 830,000 US customers but missed the main Gulf Coast refineries.
  • US gas — output peaked near 113 billion cubic feet a day; one oil research firm expects a temporary glut in 2027.
  • LPG — the UAE overtook the US as India's top LPG supplier in September as Gulf shipments recovered.

Most listed companies are far below their highs and valuations have reset, but foreign investors are selling at a record pace and the bounce has not spread wide.

05

India's economy: evening power runs short, festive shoppers turn up, and US friction grows

Solar power floods the grid by day and vanishes at dusk, online festive sales started strongly, and the US hit India's biggest IT employers while India and Elon Musk traded words.

In short
  • India is short of electricity in the evening, so power companies are paying up to ₹20 a unit.
  • Festive online sales opened with record traffic, much of it from small towns.

The evening power gap became a price problem
